Why this started

It began with a 7am message check.

The idea came from the creator’s partner, a student substitute teacher working with one school: checking for a text each weekday morning to find out whether she was needed.

That experience prompted this project. It is one person’s reported situation, not evidence that every school works this way. No identifying details or school records are used here.

“Am I needed tomorrow, and what will I be teaching?”

Invented problem statement, based on the reported experience. Not a testimonial or a direct quote.
THE INITIAL CUSTOMER SEGMENT

Irish secondary schools coordinating day-to-day cover with a regular panel of substitutes.

The proposed first users are the cover coordinator and student substitute teachers who are eligible for the work offered.

Who decides and pays?
A principal or school management body approves a school licence. A national education body would evaluate and procure any wider rollout.

How it is handled today
The reported case relies on school-to-teacher texts. A working assumption is that cover, lesson notes and staff queries can end up across messages and separate records; this needs checking with school coordinators.

The promise is clearer coordination. The app cannot predict absences or guarantee work before a school knows it needs cover.
A school operations portal

One connected journey,
from available to prepared.

Sub Teaching is a web-based coordination and planning portal. The demo shows the school and teacher sides of the same workflow.

01

Set the teaching context.

The teacher first chooses one school or multiple schools, adds subjects and sets one-hour availability between 09:00 and 16:00. Lunch, 13:00–14:00, is blocked. Teachers linked to one school can opt into a selected local network.

02

Offer cover. Confirm the response.

The coordinator posts one lesson and selects one or more relevant teachers. Matching checks school links, subjects, availability and clashes. The first acceptance confirms the booking and closes the other invitations for that job.

03

Prepare and follow through.

Accepted lessons appear in My week, alongside recurring classes and linked lesson plans. Teachers can track school correspondence, worked hours and illustrative expected earnings.

Try the complete task: choose School in “Demo view” → create a future cover offer → choose Teacher → accept → open the timetable → save a lesson plan → refresh.

SCHOOL PRICE RATIONALE

A small, testable annual commitment.

At an assumed €25 per hour of coordinator time, €360 equates to 14.4 hours a year—24 minutes per week over an assumed 36-week working period. That is a break-even target for a pilot to measure, not a result achieved.

Illustrative school-service cost envelope

At a hypothetical 100 paying schools, revenue would be €36,000/year: €6,000 hosting and tools + €12,000 maintenance + €9,000 onboarding/support + €9,000 reserve. These are unquoted planning allowances, suitable only as a small, founder-led pilot model; they do not demonstrate funding for a full-time team. Validate support demand, costs, taxes and willingness to pay before setting a real price.

NATIONAL PRICE RATIONALE

Buy a shared service, with a defined scope.

For illustration, if 1,000 schools participated, €250,000 would equal €250 per school per year, about 31% below separate €360 licences. This is a hypothetical scenario, not a count of Irish schools or a rollout commitment.

Illustrative national annual budget

€100,000 development/maintenance + €60,000 onboarding/support + €25,000 hosting/tools + €30,000 security/accessibility assurance + €35,000 contingency = €250,000. These are assumptions, not supplier quotes or evidence of adequate capacity. A buyer would need a scoped pilot, service levels, procurement review and a separately costed implementation plan. At fewer than 695 schools, this flat fee would exceed buying €360 licences individually; the national route would need to justify any extra service value.

REAL-PERSON TEST · FEEDBACK RECORDED

A phone test changed the teacher workspace.

Feedback on the first version, supplied by the project owner. The findings below informed the mobile redesign. A follow-up test of the revised layout has not been recorded.

What they tried

The tester used the first version on a phone and compared its layout with the laptop view. They reported that the features worked, but the interface was crowded and difficult to navigate.

What broke or confused them

There were too many tabs on the phone, making the teacher workspace difficult to navigate and less visually appealing.

What changed

The mobile layout was simplified and related teacher pages were combined. Lesson planning moved from a separate tab into the weekly overview, so teachers can prepare alongside their timetable.

Builder checks completed on the demo: hourly availability, lunch exclusions, relevant teacher suggestions, multi-recipient invitations, first-acceptance booking, cancellation, earnings calculations, teacher-record separation and existing school/Summer Work workflows. These checks do not replace independent testing.

Questions school leaders should ask

Clear about the prototype.
Clear about the next step.

Will this stop teachers having to check their phones at 7am?

It aims to make confirmed work and pending offers easier to see. It cannot remove uncertainty when a school only learns of an absence that morning. The prototype has in-app alerts only; background notifications are planned.

Can a student teacher use it for substitute work?

The concept includes student substitutes, but access to a demo does not establish eligibility to teach or supervise examinations. Schools and the relevant organisers would need to verify suitability and applicable requirements before offering real work.

Are the teacher and school accounts private?

No. “Demo view” switches fictional perspectives on the same browser records; it is not authentication. The demo saves data on that browser only. A real service needs verified accounts, controlled access and secure shared storage.

Can an examination body recruit supervisors through it today?

You can try a fictional application, organiser decision and teacher acceptance. No real examination body is connected. Official recruitment integration, verification and deployment agreements are planned, not delivered.

Why would a school pay when recruitment alternatives exist?

The paid-service hypothesis is that linked lesson preparation, ongoing designated-school correspondence and seasonal coordination add value around recruitment. The pricing is untested. A school pilot should compare actual time saved, usability and cost against its current approach and existing services.

What would change the recommendation to build this?

If school coordinators find that existing services already meet these combined needs, or a pilot shows little value beyond free alternatives, the scope and paid model should change. Teacher feedback, coordinator interviews and real operating costs should come before any rollout.
